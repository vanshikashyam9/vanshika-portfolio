01  /  where this started

It kept forgetting.

I started my Computer Science degree in 2023, which was the same year these tools landed in front of me. Before that I’d never had anything like them. They were genuinely useful — for studying, for picking up skills faster than a textbook would let me, for getting unstuck at 1 a.m.

But something happened in the long conversations. Past a certain point the answers went strange. It would contradict what it had told me an hour earlier, lose the thread of what we were building, get confidently wrong about things it had been right about at the top of the chat.

Most people close the tab and open a fresh one. I wanted to know what had actually changed between the good answers and the bad ones.

Attention is a probability distribution. A distribution over more things is a thinner distribution.

That turned out to be a door into mathematics I was already learning to love. Every token in the context competes for a fixed budget of attention — the softmax normalises each row to sum to one. Add more context and every earlier token’s share gets smaller. The instruction you gave at the start isn’t deleted. It’s diluted.

That’s the simplified version, and I know it. Real degradation is also positional encodings extrapolating past the range they were trained on, quantisation error compounding in a long KV cache, and instruction drift. But the softmax intuition was the door in, and I’ve been reading through it ever since.

It also settled a question I’d been carrying since I picked my degree: I didn’t want to only use the tools. I wanted to be able to explain them.

αᵢ = exp(sᵢ) / Σⱼ exp(sⱼ). Hold the scores equal and every token’s share is exactly 1/n. Real attention is sharper than uniform — that’s the point of it — but the ceiling falls the same way, and nothing you said early on is competing on equal footing by token two thousand.

03  /  selected work

Things I’ve actually shipped.

Full-stack · data · 2025

RentScout AI

PythonFastAPI PostgreSQLSQLAlchemy Next.js 16TypeScript TailwindDocker GitHub Actions

Before you send that deposit, check the listing.

Vancouver’s rental market runs on scams that target people who don’t know what rent should cost, don’t know their tenant rights, and need housing urgently. I was one of those people. Every rental site shows you listings; none of them help you judge one. RentScout takes a listing you found anywhere — Craigslist, Marketplace, a WhatsApp group — and tells you whether it holds up.

Scam screen
Weighted signals across payment red flags, manufactured urgency, and prices benchmarked against the cheapest real listings in that specific neighbourhood — not a flat threshold. Every flag is explained in plain language.
Market data
Real CMHC Rental Market Survey vacancy rates, 29 Metro Vancouver zones, 2022–2025, each with a plain-English read: “tight market, move fast” vs “room to negotiate.”
Budget planner
Live verdict from income and expenses — comfortable, tight, or financially risky — with a warning when rent crosses the 30%-of-income guideline.
Engineering
Dockerised backend and frontend, JWT + bcrypt auth, deterministic seed data, and CI running lint, typecheck, build, and API smoke tests on every push.

The decision I’m proudest of

There is no legal way to bulk-scrape Craigslist or Facebook Marketplace. I could have quietly shipped a scraper and let the demo look richer. Instead the search page runs on clearly-labelled sample data, and the product focuses on what it can honestly do: screen the listing you bring it. The market data is real. And every scan carries the same disclaimer — a clean result is not a guarantee, never pay before viewing in person.

A tool that helps people avoid being deceived cannot cut corners on what it claims about itself.

In progress: a retrieval-augmented assistant over the BC Residential Tenancy Act — ask “can my landlord raise rent mid-lease?” and get an answer with citations to the actual legislation. It’s the piece of this project that sits closest to what I want to do next.
